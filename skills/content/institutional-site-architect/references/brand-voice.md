# Brand Voice for Institutional Sites

Reference for writing the voice, positioning, and editorial tone of an institutional website. Different discipline from direct-response copy. Sources: Marty Neumeier (*Zag*, *The Brand Gap*), Ogilvy, Harvard Business Review style, Rand Fishkin, Copyhackers corporate work, Stripe Press, Apple marketing copy, Linear blog.

---

## 1. Voice vs Tone vs Style

Three distinct concepts. Getting them right is 80% of institutional writing.

- **Voice** — the consistent personality of the organization. Doesn't change. (e.g., "confident, precise, warm")
- **Tone** — the situational adjustment of voice based on context. (e.g., more formal in legal disclaimers, warmer in careers page)
- **Style** — the mechanical rules (capitalization, punctuation, numbers, product names, etc.)

Voice is WHO. Tone is HOW you sound RIGHT NOW. Style is the ground rules.

---

## 2. Positioning — The Foundation

Before voice, write the positioning. Without positioning, voice is decoration.

### 2.1 The positioning formula

> **[Org] is the [category] for [audience] that [unique differentiator].**

Examples:

- *"Linear is the issue tracking tool for software teams that value speed and craft."*
- *"Basecamp is the project management app for teams that want calm over chaos."*
- *"Stripe is the payments platform for internet businesses."*

### 2.2 Rules

- **One sentence max**
- **Specific category** — not "the platform" or "the solution"
- **Specific audience** — not "for everyone"
- **Clear differentiator** — the thing only you do well

### 2.3 Tests

- Could your competitor say the exact same thing? → rewrite
- Does the reader know WHAT you do from this sentence? → good
- Could you add "unlike X" naturally? → good differentiator
- Is it boring? → sharpen

### 2.4 Where the positioning lives

- On the homepage hero (adapted into a headline)
- On the About page as the leading paragraph
- In every press release, pitch deck, and sales script
- In the meta description of the homepage

The positioning is the skeleton every paragraph on the site hangs from.

## 3. Voice Attributes — Pick 3

Most great brand voices boil down to 3 adjectives. Any more and the voice becomes noise.

### 3.1 Pattern: two adjacent + one tension

- **Confident + warm + precise** — Stripe
- **Direct + calm + witty** — Basecamp
- **Bold + simple + playful** — Liquid Death
- **Rigorous + curious + plain-spoken** — Wirecutter
- **Thoughtful + minimal + editorial** — Apple
- **Expert + approachable + human** — Linear

The "tension" adjective prevents the voice from becoming one-note. Confident + warm is more interesting than just confident.

### 3.2 Anti-patterns

Pick 3 adjectives that are:
- Not too abstract ("innovative" means nothing)
- Not too generic ("professional" is a given)
- Not contradictory (you can't be "formal + playful + casual")
- Not a wishlist (what you ARE, not what you wish to be)

### 3.3 Write the voice chart

For each adjective, write:

| Adjective | We are | We are NOT |
|---|---|---|
| Confident | clear, firm, decisive | arrogant, bragging, dismissive |
| Warm | human, friendly, caring | cheesy, over-eager, saccharine |
| Precise | specific, evidence-backed, technical when needed | pedantic, cold, jargon-heavy |

This chart is what new writers use to check their drafts.

## 4. Tone Calibration — Situational Voice

Voice stays constant. Tone flexes by context.

### 4.1 Tone matrix

| Context | Tone shift |
|---|---|
| **Home page** | Confident, aspirational, broad |
| **About page** | Warm, personal, story-driven |
| **Case studies** | Evidence-led, specific, factual |
| **Blog / thought leadership** | Expert, curious, opinionated |
| **Careers page** | Warm, human, inviting |
| **Contact page** | Friendly, low-friction |
| **Legal / terms** | Clear, formal, no jargon tricks |
| **Error pages** | Light, helpful, brief |
| **Press / news** | Neutral, factual, quotable |
| **Changelog** | Technical, specific, direct |

### 4.2 The rule of matching energy

Match the energy of the context. A careers page that reads like a legal disclaimer kills recruiting. A legal page that reads like a sales pitch kills trust.

## 5. Institutional Writing Rules

### 5.1 Show, don't tell

**Tell**: "We deliver world-class results for Fortune 500 companies."
**Show**: "Acme Corp cut their onboarding time from 14 days to 4 hours using our tool. They're one of 30 Fortune 500 clients."

Institutional writing earns authority through specificity. Vague claims destroy it.

### 5.2 Specific > superlative

**Bad**: "The best platform for growing businesses"
**Good**: "The platform trusted by 4,200 B2B SaaS companies between $1M and $50M ARR"

Numbers, names, constraints — these signal that you know your audience and your own boundaries.

### 5.3 Shorter sentences

The average American reads at an 8th-grade level. Complex sentences signal effort without clarity.

**Bad**: "Our comprehensive platform leverages advanced AI capabilities to deliver enterprise-grade solutions that drive measurable business outcomes across the entire customer lifecycle."

**Good**: "We use AI to automate customer support. Our customers resolve 70% of tickets without a human. Here's how it works."

### 5.4 Active voice

- **Passive**: "Results are delivered by our team."
- **Active**: "Our team delivers results."

Passive voice hides the subject — and institutional writing needs to name the subject (who did it, who will do it).

Exception: use passive when you *need* to obscure the subject (legal reasons, neutrality in news).

### 5.5 "We" and "You"

- **"We"** — speaks as the organization, warm and direct
- **"Our team"** — slightly more formal
- **"[Company name]"** — most formal, third-person (rarely use)
- **"You"** — addresses the reader directly (warmer than "users" or "customers")

Ratio: "you" should outnumber "we" by at least 2:1 on customer-facing pages. Otherwise you're talking about yourself, not to them.

### 5.6 Concrete details beat adjectives

- **Adjective**: "A highly experienced team"
- **Detail**: "A team with 12 years' average experience, including former engineers from Google, Stripe, and Meta"

Details paint. Adjectives blur.

## 6. Banned Vendehumo Words

Same list as landing-page-architect, adapted for corporate context:

**Always banned**:
revolutionary, game-changing, disruptive, next-gen, cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, state-of-the-art, paradigm shift, transformative, innovative (unless you specify HOW), unprecedented, pioneering, leading, premier, trusted, unique (unless you prove it), synergy, leverage, unlock, supercharge, 10x, ninja, rockstar, guru, seamless, frictionless, effortless, thought leader, value-add, actionable insights, robust, holistic, end-to-end, turnkey, solution

**Context-dependent bans** (use carefully, with evidence):
- "Proven" → okay if you show proof
- "Expert" → okay if you show credentials
- "Leader" → okay if you have quantifiable market position
- "Award-winning" → okay if you name the awards
- "Innovative" → okay if you describe the innovation

**Rule**: If you can't immediately answer "how?" or "prove it", delete the word.

## 7. Writing for Each Page Type

### 7.1 Hero copy (home, landing-style sections)

- **Headline**: what you do, for whom, how. Max 10 words. Specific.
- **Subhead**: expand the headline with the "how" or "for whom"
- **CTA**: action + value

**Bad hero**: "Transforming the future of work"
**Good hero**: "The project management tool for teams who ship weekly — not whenever"

### 7.2 About page

- Lead with WHY — why you exist, what problem you saw
- Then HOW — your approach, philosophy, method
- Then WHO — team, founders, credentials (third, not first)
- NEVER lead with "Founded in 2015, Acme is a..."

Template:

> We started [Company] because [frustration with the status quo]. We believe [conviction]. Every day we [what you do, specifically]. Our team of [number] does this from [places].

### 7.3 Services / What we do

For each service / offering:

- **Clear name** — not jargon
- **1-line description** — what it is
- **Who it's for** — specific audience
- **What you get** — tangible deliverable or outcome
- **Proof point** — one metric, one quote, one case

### 7.4 Case studies

Classic pattern: **Challenge → Approach → Outcome**

- **Challenge**: the specific problem the client faced
- **Approach**: what you did (skim-level, not proprietary)
- **Outcome**: numbers, quotes, artifacts

Each case study ends with a one-line call: "See more cases" or "Talk to us about a similar project."

### 7.5 Team / People page

- **Photo** — real, consistent style, natural not stocky
- **Name** — full
- **Role** — clear, not bloated ("VP of Strategic Partnerships") unless accurate
- **One-line bio** — specific credential + human detail
- **Optional**: link to LinkedIn, personal site, Twitter

Example:
> **Elena Ruiz** — Head of Engineering
> Former staff engineer at Stripe. Built our platform from day one. Cyclist, dog person, sourdough obsessive.

### 7.6 Careers page

- Lead with **why work here** — mission, values, stage
- Show **how you work** — remote / hybrid / office, async, tools, rituals
- Show **benefits** — honest, specific
- List **open roles** with location, type, short description + link
- End with **culture proof** — photos, testimonials from team, press/awards related to culture

**NEVER**: "We're like a family", "Fast-paced environment", "Ninja wanted"

### 7.7 Contact page

- **Direct contact methods** — email address (written out), phone if relevant, address if physical
- **Short form** — name + email + message, nothing else unless you must
- **Expected response time** — "We respond within 1 business day"
- **FAQ** — if you get the same questions often
- **Map** — if physical location matters

### 7.8 Press / Media page

- **Boilerplate description** — 1 paragraph you want press to copy-paste
- **Founder / exec bios** — short
- **Brand assets** — logos, photos, color palette for download
- **Past coverage** — links to articles
- **Press contact** — direct email to a human
- **News releases** — reverse chronological

### 7.9 Legal pages

- **Plain language** — not "whereas the party of the first part"
- **Clear headings** — so readers can find specific clauses
- **Updated date** — prominent
- **Contact for questions**
- **Version history** — for major terms changes

Good example: Basecamp's policies. Worth studying.

### 7.10 Blog / Insights

- **No "Thoughts" or "Ramblings"** — name it what it is: Blog, Insights, Journal, Notes, Writing
- **Clear post titles** — tell me the content, not tease it
- **Author + date** — every post
- **Reading time** — helps commitment decisions
- **Categories / tags** — if > 20 posts
- **RSS feed** — yes (still useful)

## 8. Editorial Rhythm

### 8.1 Vary length

Short. Then medium. Then a longer, flowing sentence that lets the reader breathe and settles the point. Short again.

Bad rhythm = all sentences the same length. Reads like a textbook.

### 8.2 One idea per paragraph

If a paragraph has two ideas, split it. Visual rhythm on the page matches mental rhythm.

### 8.3 Read it aloud

If you stumble, rewrite. If you get bored, cut. If it sounds like a human telling another human something, you're done.

### 8.4 Use transitions — but sparingly

Good transitions: "Here's the thing." / "But." / "So." / "And then."
Bad transitions: "Furthermore." / "Additionally." / "In conclusion."

## 9. Style Rules (decisions to make and document)

### 9.1 Capitalization

- **Title case** or **sentence case** for headings? (Pick one. Sentence case is warmer; title case is more formal.)
- **Product names** — exact casing always (Next.js not NextJS)
- **Company name** — consistent across the site
- **Job titles** — capitalized when used as titles ("Elena Ruiz, Head of Engineering") but not in running text ("she is head of engineering")

### 9.2 Punctuation

- **Oxford comma** — yes or no? (I recommend yes, always.)
- **Em dash** — yes, use it (`—` not ` - `)
- **Ellipsis** — use sparingly
- **Quotes** — curly (`" "`) not straight (`" "`) in prose

### 9.3 Numbers

- **Spell out** one through nine; **numerals** for 10+
- **Consistency** in prose vs marketing (marketing can break rules for punch)
- **Currency** — with symbol, no space: `$99` not `$ 99`
- **Dates** — ISO for metadata, human for prose (`April 11, 2026` or `11 April 2026`)

### 9.4 Links

- **Descriptive anchor text** — "Read the case study" not "click here"
- **External links** — subtly marked (icon or new tab)
- **Internal links** — same tab by default

### 9.5 Inclusive language

- **Gender-neutral** — "they" is singular and accepted
- **Avoid ableist language** ("crazy", "insane" — use sparingly)
- **International readers** — watch for idioms that don't translate
- **Accessibility** — alt text for every meaningful image

## 10. Multilingual Writing

If the site is multilingual:

- **Translate, don't auto-translate** — machine translation breaks voice
- **Hire native speakers** — not bilinguals, native
- **Localize, don't just translate** — culture, references, examples
- **Fallback language** — clear default if translation missing
- **URL structure** — `/es/about` or `es.example.com/about` (pick one, stick to it)
- **Language switcher** — visible, clear (not a flag icon alone — flags ≠ languages)
- **Date / currency / number formats** — localize
- **RTL support** — for Arabic, Hebrew if applicable

## 11. Brand Voice Anti-Patterns

- **Corporate-speak** — "At Acme, we pride ourselves on delivering..."
- **Mission-statement headlines** — "Empowering the future of work"
- **First-person-plural overload** — every sentence starts with "We"
- **Superlative stacking** — "world-class best-in-class leading premier"
- **Jargon walls** — industry terms only insiders know
- **Cheap emotion** — "Imagine a world where..."
- **Generic inclusivity** — "Everyone deserves..."
- **Fake humility** — "We're just a small team trying to..."
- **Overconfident claims** — "The only [X] you'll ever need"
- **Cheap humor** — jokes that don't fit the brand
- **Inconsistent voice** — warm on home, cold on pricing, stiff in legal, unfunny on 404
- **Emoji as personality** — one or two, intentional, never as a crutch
- **"!" stacking** — excitement via punctuation
- **Buzzword bingo** — AI-powered, blockchain-enabled, leveraging synergies
- **"We're like Uber for X"** — lazy positioning
- **No point of view** — wishy-washy, trying not to offend anyone

## 12. Brand Voice Checklist

- [ ] Positioning statement written (one sentence)
- [ ] 3 voice adjectives picked
- [ ] Voice chart (we are / we are not) for each adjective
- [ ] Tone matrix for context shifts (home vs legal vs careers)
- [ ] "You" : "We" ratio ≥ 2:1 on customer-facing pages
- [ ] No banned vendehumo words
- [ ] Every claim backed by specificity or proof
- [ ] Active voice default
- [ ] Style guide decisions (capitalization, punctuation, numbers)
- [ ] Sample transformations (before/after) for new writers
- [ ] Rhythm tested (read aloud)
- [ ] Multilingual plan if applicable
- [ ] Inclusive language reviewed
