# Trust & Authority for Institutional Sites

How institutional sites build credibility. Direct-response landing pages sell; institutional sites **earn trust**. Different levers, different placements.

Sources: Cialdini (*Influence*), Stephen Covey (*Speed of Trust*), Nielsen Norman research on e-commerce trust, real-world analysis of high-trust brands (Stripe, Linear, Patagonia, Basecamp, Y Combinator).

---

## 1. The Trust Equation

> **Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation**

- **Credibility** — do you know what you're talking about? (credentials, expertise, proof)
- **Reliability** — do you deliver? (track record, consistency)
- **Intimacy** — do you understand me? (empathy, specific audience knowledge)
- **Self-orientation** — how much is this about you vs about me? (the denominator — lower is better)

Every page on an institutional site should nudge this equation in your favor.

---

## 2. The Seven Trust Signals — Ranked by Power

From strongest to weakest:

1. **Specific outcomes with named clients** — *"Helped Acme reduce onboarding from 14 days to 4 hours"*
2. **Quoted testimonials with photos and full attribution** — real humans vouching
3. **Case studies with numbers and process artifacts** — evidence of methodology
4. **Client logos** — social proof by association
5. **Named press coverage** — *"Featured in TechCrunch, WSJ"*
6. **Awards and certifications** — third-party validation
7. **Years in business + team size** — implicit stability

**Rule**: stack multiple signals at the top of the funnel (home), layer in deep evidence mid-funnel (case studies, about), close with specific guarantees or contact at the bottom (CTAs).

## 3. Where Each Signal Lives

Not every proof element belongs everywhere. Map them to the right pages.

### Home page
- Logos bar (6-12 recognizable names)
- Hero adjacent: one statistic or one-line testimonial
- Featured case study (1-2 max)
- Press mentions strip
- Optional: investor logos (if that's part of credibility for the audience)

### About page
- Founding story (specific, human)
- Team credentials (background, notable prior work)
- Years in business, team size, countries
- Investors (if relevant)
- Advisory board (if mature org)
- Awards and recognition

### Services / What we do
- Per-service: one proof point (metric, quote, link to case)
- Case study carousel at the bottom

### Work / Cases
- The whole page is proof — deep case studies with numbers

### Team page
- Individual credentials — prior companies, publications, talks, certifications
- Personal details (human trust)

### Careers
- Employee testimonials
- Great Place to Work / Glassdoor ratings (if strong)
- Awards related to culture

### Press page
- Press coverage list
- Boilerplate for consistency
- Awards

### Footer
- Certifications (SOC 2, ISO, GDPR-compliant)
- Industry association logos
- Years in business ("Since 2015")

## 4. Client Logos — The First Line of Defense

### 4.1 Rules

- **6-12 logos** — fewer feels thin, more becomes a wall
- **Recognizable names first** — brand value scales with recognition
- **Monochrome** — usually looks more premium and avoids color chaos
- **Consistent size and spacing**
- **Industry mix** — varied if you serve many, focused if you're a specialist
- **Grayscale fade on hover** — subtle interaction

### 4.2 When you don't have big names

- **Segment count** — *"Trusted by 340 B2B SaaS companies"*
- **Industry coverage** — *"Used by teams at 8 of the top 25 [industry] companies"*
- **Use case proof** — *"Processes $2B in payments annually"*

Never use fake logos. Never use logos without permission (it's legally and ethically bad).

### 4.3 Attribution tiers

- **"Trusted by"** — vague but common
- **"Our customers"** — clearer
- **"As used by"** — implies usage, not just trial
- **"Partnered with"** — strongest if true

## 5. Testimonials That Work

### 5.1 What makes a testimonial credible

- **Full name** — first + last
- **Role + company** — specific, verifiable
- **Photo** — real, not stock
- **Specific outcome** — numbers, before/after
- **Natural voice** — not over-polished marketing-speak
- **Link to LinkedIn or case** — lets skeptics verify

### 5.2 The anatomy

Weak:
> "Amazing product! Highly recommended." — J.D.

Strong:
> "We were losing 40% of leads to slow response time. After switching to Acme, our average first-response dropped from 6 hours to 12 minutes. Pipeline doubled in a quarter."
> — **Maria Chen**, Head of Sales, Acme Corp
> [photo] [link to LinkedIn]

### 5.3 Placement

- **Home page** — 1-2 punchy quotes
- **Services page** — 1 per service (right where the claim is made)
- **Case studies** — full-length quote inside the case
- **Pricing page** — 1 before the CTA
- **Footer** — rating bar if strong (G2, Capterra, TrustPilot)

### 5.4 Video testimonials

Massively stronger than written — but only if:
- Real clients (not actors)
- Under 90 seconds
- Subtitled (80% watched muted)
- Specific story (not generic praise)

Use video sparingly — 1-3 on the site, in the highest-impact spots.

### 5.5 Anti-patterns

- Anonymous quotes ("A happy customer")
- Over-polished marketing-speak
- First-name-only ("John D.")
- Stock photo testimonials (obvious and brand-destroying)
- All testimonials generic ("great service", "highly recommend")
- 50 testimonials in a wall (diminishing returns)

## 6. Case Studies as the Deep Proof Layer

Case studies are where institutional trust gets built at depth. A prospect reading a case study is almost a buyer — they just need proof.

### 6.1 Structure (Challenge → Approach → Outcome — CAO)

**Challenge**: What the client faced. Specific. Numbers where possible.
**Approach**: What you did. Skimmable — don't hide your method. Show, don't obscure.
**Outcome**: Results. Numbers, quotes, before/after artifacts.

### 6.2 Ingredients of a great case study

- **Real client name** — or explicit permission to redact ("A Fortune 500 retailer")
- **Industry context** — helps readers self-identify
- **Specific problem** — quantified pain
- **Process transparency** — how you worked, not just what you delivered
- **Before/after metrics** — the center of gravity
- **Client quote** — strong if it's specific
- **Team credit** — who worked on it
- **Duration and scope** — timeline, budget range
- **Visual artifacts** — screenshots, diagrams, photos
- **Related cases** — 2-3 similar at the bottom

### 6.3 Length

- **Short case** (400-600 words) — quick scan, good for volume
- **Medium case** (800-1500 words) — the sweet spot
- **Long case** (2000+ words) — for flagship cases, enterprise buyers

### 6.4 Gated vs ungated

- **Ungated** — more readers, better SEO, better trust
- **Gated** — fewer readers, captures leads, acceptable for premium content

**Default**: ungate. Gating case studies feels like hiding your proof.

### 6.5 Case study anti-patterns

- Vague outcomes ("improved efficiency")
- Missing numbers
- Anonymized clients when there's no real reason (weakens trust)
- Hidden behind a form wall
- Generic process diagrams
- Over-polished, under-specific
- Written like marketing collateral, not a story
- Missing client quote

## 7. Numbers and Metrics

Specificity is the friend of trust. Generic claims are its enemy.

### 7.1 What numbers to show

- **Clients / customers**: "4,200 teams", "340 B2B SaaS companies"
- **Usage / volume**: "$2B processed", "1.5M requests/day", "12M messages sent"
- **Longevity**: "Since 2015", "12 years of shipping"
- **Team**: "40 engineers", "Team of 12 designers and developers"
- **Geography**: "Teams in 8 countries", "Clients in 34 countries"
- **Outcomes**: "Average 3x ROI", "Customers save 12 hours/week"
- **Quality**: "99.98% uptime", "NPS 72"

### 7.2 Rules

- **Specific, not round** — "4,273" is more credible than "over 4,000" in some contexts; "4,200+" works if you want breathing room
- **Updated** — stale numbers kill trust ("Since 2015, we've served 1,000+ customers" when it's 2026 and you have 10,000 → update)
- **Context** — a number alone ("$2B") is weaker than framed ("$2B processed annually, 30% YoY growth")
- **Verifiable** — if challenged, can you prove it?
- **Not inflated** — don't count trials as customers, don't count GitHub stars as users

### 7.3 Where numbers live

- **Hero adjacent** — one or two headline numbers
- **About page** — stats bar
- **Case studies** — outcome metrics
- **Services** — proof points
- **Footer** — years in business

## 8. Founder / Team Credibility

For many orgs (agencies, law firms, consultancies, personal brands), trust comes from individuals.

### 8.1 What to show

- **Prior work** — notable companies, projects, publications
- **Credentials** — degrees (if premium matters), certifications, bar membership
- **Publications** — books, articles, papers
- **Speaking** — conferences, podcasts, talks
- **Press** — quoted in, interviewed by
- **Community** — open source contributions, industry leadership
- **Awards** — specific, named, recent

### 8.2 How to present

- **Photo** — professional but human (not LinkedIn corporate stock)
- **Short bio** — specific credential + human detail
- **Full bio** (optional) — longer version on individual profile page
- **Links** — LinkedIn, personal site, Twitter, publications

### 8.3 Anti-patterns

- Everyone is "passionate about X"
- Vague titles ("Thought Leader")
- No individual detail (templates)
- Stock or outdated photos
- Missing contact method (for customer-facing roles)
- Every bio identical in structure and tone

## 9. Press & Media Coverage

Press coverage is borrowed credibility.

### 9.1 Tiers

- **Tier 1**: Major outlets (NYT, WSJ, Financial Times, TechCrunch, WIRED, The Verge, Bloomberg)
- **Tier 2**: Industry-leading publications (for your niche)
- **Tier 3**: Niche blogs, podcasts, trade publications
- **Tier 4**: Small mentions, press release syndication

### 9.2 How to display

- **"As featured in" logo strip** on home page — only for real, verifiable features
- **Press page** — full list of coverage with links
- **Specific quotes** — pull out the most impactful quote from the article

### 9.3 Anti-patterns

- Logos of outlets you were NOT actually covered in
- Outdated features (5+ years old with nothing recent looks dead)
- Paid placements presented as earned coverage (the internet will find out)
- Generic "Featured in Forbes" when it was a sponsored post

## 10. Awards & Certifications

### 10.1 What to show

- **Industry awards** — specific, recent, verifiable
- **Security / compliance** — SOC 2, ISO 27001, GDPR-compliant, HIPAA-compliant
- **Quality certifications** — ISO 9001, etc.
- **Vendor badges** — Google Partner, AWS Partner, Apple Developer, etc.
- **Community recognition** — G2 Leader, Capterra Top Performer, Best Place to Work

### 10.2 Placement

- **Footer** — certification and compliance badges (always visible)
- **About page** — achievements section
- **Home page** — 1-2 most prestigious if space allows
- **Press page** — complete list

### 10.3 Anti-patterns

- Awards from 2012 as the most recent
- Too many (wall of badges dilutes impact)
- Badges for obscure awards nobody recognizes
- Outdated certifications (SOC 2 expired 2 years ago — fraud)

## 11. Compliance as Trust Signal

For B2B and regulated industries, compliance IS trust.

### 11.1 Common certifications

- **SOC 2 Type II** — for B2B SaaS, now table stakes
- **ISO 27001** — international security standard
- **GDPR** — EU data protection (required if you have EU users)
- **CCPA** — California privacy
- **HIPAA** — US healthcare
- **PCI-DSS** — payment card industry
- **FedRAMP** — US government
- **HITRUST** — healthcare-adjacent

### 11.2 Show these

- **Footer badges** with link to trust/security page
- **Dedicated "Security" or "Trust" page** with details
- **On pricing page** — reassurance for enterprise buyers
- **In SOC 2 report** — provide on request with NDA

### 11.3 The Trust / Security page

Larger orgs have a dedicated `/security` or `/trust` page with:
- Infrastructure overview
- Data handling practices
- Encryption details
- Access controls
- Audit summaries (not full SOC 2 report — that's on request)
- Compliance badges
- Bug bounty program
- Security contact

Stripe's and Vercel's trust pages are good references.

## 12. Social Proof Momentum

Trust compounds. Show the momentum.

### 12.1 "Loved by X" patterns

- *"Loved by 4,200 teams"*
- *"Used by 30% of Y Combinator's top startups"*
- *"Chosen by 8 of the 10 largest [industry] companies"*

### 12.2 Growth indicators

- *"Doubling every year"*
- *"Shipped 120 updates in 2025"*
- *"Hiring 20+ engineers this year"*

### 12.3 Ecosystem proof

- **Integrations count** — *"Integrates with 200+ tools"*
- **Partners count** — *"800+ certified partners"*
- **Community size** — *"20k Slack community"*
- **Open source** — *"15k GitHub stars"* (only if it matters)

## 13. Founder Letters and Personal Touches

For startups and personal brands: founder voice.

### 13.1 The founder letter

A letter from the founder explaining why the org exists, on the About page or as a dedicated piece. Works when:
- The org is still small / early
- The founder has a distinctive voice
- The story is genuinely interesting

Avoid when:
- The org is large and the founder is distant from day-to-day
- The letter would be ghostwritten corporate-speak
- It doesn't add specific value over a standard About page

### 13.2 Personal elements

- Signed email from founder (for small orgs)
- Founder photo on home page (for agencies)
- Founder's story in the About
- Founder's hand-drawn illustration (niche but memorable)

## 14. Anti-Trust Signals (remove immediately)

- **Stock photos of smiling diverse teams with laptops** (everyone sees these as fake)
- **Generic mission statements** ("Empowering tomorrow's leaders")
- **Vague claims** ("industry-leading", "best-in-class")
- **Outdated copyright year** (shows abandonment)
- **Broken links** in the footer
- **Missing contact info**
- **No About page** or About page with no team
- **"About Us" that's actually a sales pitch**
- **Fake testimonials** (anonymized or generic)
- **Inconsistent formatting** across pages
- **Typos and grammar errors**
- **SSL certificate warnings**
- **No dark mode when your audience expects it** (developers especially)
- **Hidden pricing** for low-ticket products (acceptable for enterprise)
- **Excessive pop-ups**
- **Cookie banners that dark-pattern consent**
- **No privacy policy**
- **Out-of-date blog posts** as "recent"
- **Certifications without dates or with expired dates**

## 15. The Trust Stack by Stage

### Early-stage (0-50 employees)

- Founder story
- Specific credentials of small team
- Initial client logos (even if small)
- Early case studies
- Investor names (if any)
- Vision and momentum

### Growth-stage (50-500 employees)

- Larger logos
- Multiple case studies
- Team depth (director-level exec bios)
- Press coverage
- Compliance certifications
- Product maturity indicators

### Enterprise-stage (500+ employees)

- Brand recognition
- Press of record
- Analyst mentions (Gartner, Forrester)
- Compliance everywhere
- Diverse case studies
- Investor returns / financials (if public)
- Governance / board

Don't try to look bigger than you are — it looks fake. Lean into your actual stage.
