# Post Types — Structure Templates

8 canonical post types. Each has a different objective, structure, and reader contract. Pick the type before writing — it determines the template.

---

## 1. How-To / Tutorial

**Objective**: teach the reader to do one specific thing.
**Reader contract**: "I'll know how to do X when I finish."
**Length**: 1000–3000 words depending on complexity.

**When to use**:
- Reader has a task to accomplish
- There's a clear before/after (can't do → can do)
- You have genuine expertise or a tested process

**Structure**:
1. **Hook** — name the problem or the task the reader arrived to solve (1-2 sentences)
2. **What you'll achieve** — explicit outcome statement ("By the end, you'll have X")
3. **Prerequisites** — what the reader needs before starting (tools, skills, accounts, time)
4. **Steps** — numbered, imperative verbs. Each step: action + why + expected result
5. **Troubleshooting** — top 2-3 things that break and how to fix them
6. **Result / recap** — what success looks like
7. **What's next** — natural next tutorial in the sequence (or CTA)

**Copy rules**:
- Steps: short sentences, one action each
- Screenshots or code examples are mandatory if the procedure is visual/technical
- If a step depends on a choice (A or B), fork it explicitly — don't collapse ambiguity
- Every step should be verifiable — reader knows if they did it right

**Anti-patterns**:
- Steps that assume knowledge not covered in prerequisites
- "It's easy!" (it's easy for you)
- No troubleshooting section
- Steps numbered 1, 2, 3... 17 — break into sub-sections at > 7 steps
- Ending without showing the finished result

## 2. Listicle

**Objective**: pack a lot of value fast. High scanability.
**Reader contract**: "I'll leave with N concrete things."
**Length**: 800–2000 words.

**When to use**:
- Multiple distinct points with roughly equal weight
- Reader is in browse mode, not deep-study mode
- Topic has a natural enumerable structure

**Structure**:
1. **Hook + promise** — "Here are the 7 [X] that [outcome]" — be specific about the number
2. **Brief context** — why this list matters / who it's for (2-4 sentences max)
3. **Items** — each item gets: bold header + 2-5 sentences of substance. NOT single-sentence bullets.
4. **Closing takeaway** — 1 paragraph synthesizing the pattern across items, or a decision framework

**Copy rules**:
- Odd numbers (7, 9, 11) outperform even in click-through — use when natural
- Each item must be self-contained (reader can start at #4)
- Lead each item with the most useful sentence — no preamble per item
- If items are ranked, say so ("ordered by impact" / "random order — pick your starting point")
- No filler items to hit a number. 7 strong > 12 padded.

**Anti-patterns**:
- Single-word or single-sentence items (not an article — it's a tweet)
- No synthesis — 11 bullets then it ends
- Fake ranking ("the top 10" that are actually alphabetical)
- All items the same length and depth — vary based on importance

## 3. Opinion / POV

**Objective**: argue a clear position. Move the reader's thinking.
**Reader contract**: "I'll understand why the author believes X and whether I agree."
**Length**: 600–1500 words.

**When to use**:
- You have a genuine, specific, defensible view
- The conventional wisdom is wrong or incomplete
- You want to establish thought leadership on a topic

**Structure**:
1. **The claim** — your position, stated plainly, in the first paragraph. No build-up.
2. **The contrast** — what most people believe or do instead (and why it's wrong/incomplete)
3. **Your argument** — evidence, logic, examples. 2-4 supporting pillars.
4. **The counterargument** — steelman the opposing view, then rebut
5. **The implication** — what changes if the reader accepts your view

**Copy rules**:
- First paragraph = your actual claim, not context
- "I think" / "I believe" sparingly — own the claim without hedging
- Evidence hierarchy: your own data > research you've done > cited research > anecdote. Never reverse.
- Acknowledge what you don't know — it builds trust
- The rebuttal of the counterargument must be genuine — no straw men

**Anti-patterns**:
- Burying the take (reader gets to paragraph 4 before finding it)
- Opinion with no argument — "I believe X" + 10 sentences of vibes
- Fake controversy — "Hot take: sleep is important"
- Switching positions mid-post because you anticipated the pushback
- No counterargument section — selective presentation kills credibility

## 4. Case Study / Story

**Objective**: prove a result or teach through narrative.
**Reader contract**: "I'll see this approach work (or fail) in a real situation."
**Length**: 1000–3000 words.

**When to use**:
- You have real results (with permission or as your own story)
- The lesson is more credible embedded in context than stated abstractly
- You want to attract prospects who are in the same situation as the case

**Structure**:
1. **The result upfront** — don't bury the lede ("How we reduced churn 38% in 6 weeks")
2. **The situation** — who, what was broken, what was at stake. Make it specific.
3. **The diagnosis** — what actually caused the problem (often surprising)
4. **What we tried and failed** (optional but powerful) — makes the solution more credible
5. **The approach** — what was done and why
6. **The result** — specific numbers. Before/after table if applicable.
7. **What we learned** — transferable insight for the reader, not just "it worked"
8. **What's next** (optional) — what the org is doing now based on this learning

**Copy rules**:
- Real numbers. "Significant improvement" = useless. "38% reduction in 6 weeks" = credible.
- The protagonist should feel human — include frustrations, wrong turns
- Transferable insight is the whole point — make it explicit
- Testimonial quote from the client/subject (with permission) > paraphrase
- If you can't use real names, anonymize with a descriptor ("a fintech startup with 20 employees")

**Anti-patterns**:
- Vague outcomes ("we improved their marketing")
- Story without a transferable lesson
- Too much client context, not enough problem/solution
- Using it as a sales brochure (no tension, no failure, just win)
- Invented or unattributed numbers

## 5. Deep Dive / Explainer

**Objective**: build comprehensive understanding of a complex topic.
**Reader contract**: "I'll understand X at a level where I can use/teach it."
**Length**: 2000–5000 words.

**When to use**:
- Topic is complex, misunderstood, or under-explained
- Reader wants to truly understand, not just a quick answer
- You're establishing topical authority for SEO

**Structure**:
1. **TL;DR** — 3-5 bullets summarizing what the reader will learn (put at the top)
2. **Why this matters** — 1-2 paragraphs on stakes or misunderstandings
3. **Foundations** — concepts the rest of the post builds on (don't skip)
4. **Core content** — layered sections with headers, moving from simple to complex
5. **The nuance** — edge cases, exceptions, tradeoffs that simpler explanations miss
6. **Practical takeaway** — how to apply the understanding, not just have it
7. **Resources** — books, papers, tools for going deeper

**Copy rules**:
- TL;DR is mandatory — readers should know what they're getting into
- Analogies are a first-class tool — use them early for non-obvious concepts
- Each section should be independently useful (not "as we mentioned in section 3")
- Diagrams and visual aids compensate for 1000 words each — reference/suggest them
- Don't pad. If you run out of substance at 2000 words, end at 2000 words.

**Anti-patterns**:
- No TL;DR on a long post
- Definitions masquerading as explanations (defining a word ≠ explaining a concept)
- Too much beginner scaffolding before the real content
- Ending without a practical takeaway
- Skipping the nuance/edge cases — that's where deep dives prove their value

## 6. Comparison / vs.

**Objective**: help the reader choose between options.
**Reader contract**: "I'll know which one is right for me after reading this."
**Length**: 1000–2500 words.

**When to use**:
- Reader is evaluating alternatives and stuck
- You have real experience with both options
- High SEO intent ("X vs Y" is one of the highest-converting search patterns)

**Structure**:
1. **The comparison premise** — who should choose each option and why
2. **Quick verdict** — the one-paragraph answer for readers who won't go deeper
3. **Comparison table** — key criteria as rows, options as columns. Be honest.
4. **Criteria deep dives** — 4-8 criteria, each explained for both/all options
5. **The decision framework** — "Choose X if... Choose Y if..."
6. **What we recommend** — your honest pick, with caveats

**Copy rules**:
- Be honest about your position (do you benefit from recommending one?)
- No fake balance — if one is clearly better for most use cases, say so
- Criteria must be what the reader cares about, not what's easy to measure
- "It depends" is acceptable when the choice genuinely depends on context — explain the context
- Comparison tables must use the same scale for every row

**Anti-patterns**:
- Comparison with a predetermined winner disguised as fair
- Criteria cherry-picked to make one option win on all of them
- No "choose X if..." section — vague conclusion after detailed comparison
- Comparing apples and oranges with no acknowledgment
- Outdated information (version X, pricing as of 2023)

## 7. Interview / Q&A

**Objective**: extract and structure insights from an expert or interesting person.
**Reader contract**: "I'll learn from someone who knows this better than I do."
**Length**: 1000–3000 words.

**When to use**:
- The expert's credibility adds more than your own explanation
- The story requires a first-person voice
- You're building a publication or series (interviews build authority + relationships)

**Structure**:
1. **Subject intro** — who they are, why they matter, why now (2-4 sentences)
2. **Context** — brief setup on the topic being discussed (1 paragraph)
3. **Q&A** — formatted as **Q:** / **A:** blocks. Curated, not raw transcript.
4. **Key takeaways** (optional) — 3-5 bullets extracting the most actionable insights

**Copy rules**:
- Edit the answers for clarity without changing the meaning — this is always necessary
- Cut the filler answers ("That's a great question!") — keep only substance
- Group related questions — don't follow a meandering interview order
- Highlight pull quotes — bold or block-quote the 1-2 most powerful lines
- Your Q should be concise — the interviewee's A is the content

**Anti-patterns**:
- Unedited transcript dumps
- Sycophantic intros ("the brilliant visionary who")
- No editing of long-winded answers
- Asking questions the reader doesn't care about just because you were curious
- No takeaways at the end

## 8. Roundup / Curated

**Objective**: save the reader time by aggregating the best of something.
**Reader contract**: "I'll get the best [resources/tools/examples] on X without hunting."
**Length**: 800–2000 words.

**When to use**:
- There's a fragmented landscape the reader has to research themselves
- You've done the research and have genuine curation criteria
- High SEO potential ("best tools for X", "top examples of Y")

**Structure**:
1. **What you curated and why** — your selection criteria, upfront
2. **The items** — each item: name + what it is + why it's in the list + link. No more than 5 sentences per item.
3. **Honorable mentions** (optional) — what almost made it and why it didn't
4. **How to use this list** — decision guidance for different reader types

**Copy rules**:
- Your curation criteria must be explicit — "best" means nothing without a standard
- Annotated roundups (with your commentary) outperform bare link lists
- Items you've actually used > items you've only researched
- Update date in frontmatter — roundups go stale fast
- Don't include everything. If everything qualifies, nothing is curated.

**Anti-patterns**:
- No curation criteria ("here are 50 tools")
- Bare link lists without commentary
- Obvious affiliate bias with no disclosure
- Stale links (test before publishing)
- Mixing categories without labeling (free tools alongside $500/mo enterprise tools)

## Quick Selection Guide

| Goal | Post Type |
|---|---|
| Teach a process | How-To |
| Pack in multiple points | Listicle |
| Argue a position | Opinion |
| Prove with a real result | Case Study |
| Explain a complex topic | Deep Dive |
| Help someone choose | Comparison |
| Feature an expert's voice | Interview |
| Save the reader research time | Roundup |
